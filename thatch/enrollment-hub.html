Thatch – Pushing enrollment rates up

At Thatch, employers give their team a monthly healthcare budget and each employee picks their own plan. That only works if employees actually enroll — and enrollment has a hard deadline, after which someone goes uninsured for a month.

The people who can move that number are the admins, but they had little to work with: no read on how enrollment was tracking, no sense of who was stuck where, and no way to chase anyone without leaving the product.

I designed the enrollment hub as founding product designer — the surface admins use to see where enrollment stands and do something about it. Each decision below shows the option that shipped alongside the ones I explored and set aside.

Lead with the gap, not the roster

Shipped

One rate, broken into statuses

A list of employees tells an admin nothing about whether enrollment is going well. The question they actually have is simpler: how many people are covered, and how many aren’t yet?

The hub opens on one number — the enrollment rate — with the raw count beside it, then breaks that rate into where everyone is: enrolled, declined, in progress, invite expired, not started.

One glance gives both the size of the problem and its shape, which is what decides what an admin does next.

Roughly explored direction

A sortable employee table

The most direct reading of the request: every employee as a row, with a status column you can sort and filter.

It was precise and answered any individual question, but it made admins do the arithmetic. Nobody scanning thirty-seven rows can tell you the enrollment rate, and the people who most needed a nudge were indistinguishable from everyone else until you sorted for them.

Roughly explored direction

A tile per status

Five stat tiles across the top — one for each status, each with its count.

Scannable, and easy to extend. But equal-sized tiles gave every status equal weight, which flattened exactly the thing that mattered: you couldn’t see that “not started” dwarfed everything else. A single proportional bar carried that in one glance.

Put the action on the number itself

Shipped · Nudge attached to the cohort

Roughly explored direction · A dedicated reminders page

Shipped

Nudge attached to the cohort

Knowing that a quarter of the team hasn’t started is only useful if chasing them is the next click. Splitting insight and action across two surfaces is how admins quietly stop chasing.

Every status in the breakdown is a cohort you can act on, and the summary resolves them into a single line: the number of employees who need to take action, with the nudge attached to it.

Seeing the gap and closing it become the same interaction rather than two separate jobs.

Roughly explored direction

A dedicated reminders page

A separate surface for outreach: pick recipients, choose a template, review, send.

It offered far more control, and made room for scheduling and message history. But it sat one navigation hop away from the only place an admin ever realised a nudge was needed, and that hop was enough — the reminder became a task to do later rather than a thing you did while looking at the problem.

Order deadlines by consequence, and say what it is

Shipped · Grouped by time left, stakes spelled out

Roughly explored direction · A benefits calendar

Shipped

Grouped by time left, stakes spelled out

A date on its own doesn’t convey stakes. “August 25” and “uninsured for the entirety of September” are the same deadline, but only one of them gets an admin to act today.

Deadlines are grouped by how long is left, most urgent first, and each one spells out in plain language what happens to those employees if the date passes — along with the nudge for exactly that group.

Urgency is expressed as an outcome, so the most consequential cohort is the one an admin reaches first.

Roughly explored direction

A benefits calendar

Deadlines plotted on a month view, alongside plan year dates and renewal windows.

It was the clearest way to see how dates related to each other, and useful for planning ahead. But a calendar treats every square as equivalent: a deadline three days out looked exactly like one three weeks out, and the grid had nowhere to put the consequence of missing it. Admins could see the dates and still not know what to do this morning.
