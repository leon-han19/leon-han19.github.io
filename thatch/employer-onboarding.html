Thatch – Crafting the entire employer onboarding flow

A brief note on how Thatch works: instead of picking one plan for many employees with different health needs, employers set a monthly budget and each employee decides how to use it themselves. Any money not spent on insurance plans accumulates month-over-month to spend on anything HSA eligible. The employer saves time (since Thatch handles the administration) and money from not having to pay for an expensive group insurance plan that most of their employees won’t even nearly make full use of.

🚨

Problem: The previous onboarding flow didn’t make this value prop clear – it was too long, too vague, and wasn’t guided or opinionated enough.

🎯

Goal: use progressive disclosure and compelling storytelling to educate employers on the benefits of Thatch, and instil enough trust in what they were buying from us to walk through the flow and invite their employees, all on their own..

  • The employee-level impact sits directly beneath.

Single column with collapsing actions

  • Pre-set budget options, so an employer picks something that already works for their team instead of starting from a blank field.
  • Progressive disclosure keeps the common path short — “Have a specific price or plan?” stays tucked away until someone actually needs it.
  • A single column with the actions collapsing on scroll, so the controls and the impact on each employee get read in turn rather than competing for attention.

Explored

Controls and impact side by side

The obvious alternative was to sit the two halves next to each other — budget controls on one side, the impact on employees on the other, both in view at once. I went back and forth on this one for a while.

Splitting the width was what killed it. Neither side had enough room: the budget options collapsed to bare numbers, with no space for the line that explains what each one actually gets an employee. That explanation is the whole point of showing three options.

Having both halves live at once also left the employer with nowhere to look first. The single column shows less at any one moment, but what it does show is legible.

Explored

A slider from lean to generous

A slider with cost updating live as you drag, anchored by labels at each end.

It made the range legible and was genuinely enjoyable to use, but it framed the budget as a dial to fiddle with rather than a decision with defensible answers. It also buried the benchmark: nothing on screen said which point on the track was normal for a company like theirs.

Shipped

Named steps with continuous save

Onboarding needs information employers rarely have to hand — a full roster, details of current coverage, renewal dates. Any flow that demands it all in one sitting will lose people at the first gap.

The flow is broken into named steps, so it’s clear what’s done and what’s left, with progress saved continuously and an explicit way to save for later.

A step someone is blocked on becomes a pause instead of an abandoned signup.

Explored

One long autosaving form

A single scrolling page holding every field, saving quietly as you go.

It removed all the transitions and was the fastest path for anyone with their details ready. The problem was legibility of progress: with no named sections, an employer who got stuck couldn’t tell whether they were nearly finished or barely started, and the page gave them no way to see which specific piece was blocking them.

📈

Impact: A v0 with a minimal, core set of changes was released first, and already saw a 50% increase in employers seeing a quote and a 27% increase in employers sending their first invites.
